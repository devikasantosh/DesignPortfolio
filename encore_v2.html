TITANIC THE MUSICAL

THE PRE-SHOW EXPERIENCE

Encore transforms a theater lobby into part of the story of Titanic the Musical.
Through interactive luggage stations, character-driven audio, plot-driven scenic lighting, and a personalized telegram station, guests explore the lives of passengers before entering the theater.

The Design Challenge

How might we transform a theater lobby from a space people simply pass through into an experience that begins telling the story before the curtain rises?


We designed Encore, an interactive luggage exhibit that lets guests discover the lives of Titanic characters through their personal belongings, combining physical props, lighting, motion-triggered interactions, and character-driven audio.



Results

Encore ran for 9 sold-out shows, with more than 4000 guests.

My role

UX design, narrative design, experiential graphic design for props, 3D printing and soldering. I worked on the luggage stations, and Anna Kim worked on the telegram stations.

  • crafting user journeys and personas for our young and old audience, and making sure the experience feels complete even if they miss any or more of the three parts.
  • conducting extensive research into the characters' lives, who they were to others, and how they each spoke or wrote, and mimicking these to create stories for their journals, diaries, and pocketbooks.
  • creating a strong and engaging narrative for the whole experience, to ensure guests connect with these characters.
  • prop design and fabrication!
  • For the Entertainment Technology Center, I designed our team's logo, poster, and half sheet, too.

Concept

What can a suitcase tell you about the person who packed it?


Instead of recreating scenes from the musical, we designed each luggage station as a window into a character's life.

Guests could explore trunks, discover personal belongings, read letters and journals, and listen to characters tell their own stories as they packed their suitcases before boarding the ship, building an understanding of who they were before ever seeing them on stage.

The experience also transformed through the run of the production, allowing the story in the lobby to change alongside the show.


Emotional arc, and experience changing with phases of the show

Users and Challenges

Challenges

  • encouraging guests to touch and interact with the exhibit,
  • creating user journeys that feel complete even if the guest misses one or two phases of the experience, and
  • maintaining a strong narrative with lighting design for the one matinee show we have.

Audience personas

Mostly older audience (50+) and students - observed during playtests of other shows in the same lobby.
According to client and frequent audience faculty members, we are to expect a greater ratio of older audience for this show.

User Journey

Interactions

As the user walks past a luggage station, a motion sensor detects their presence and the trunk opens, with a programmed light tracing the outlines of the props inside, encouraging them to interact with it. We would use directional speakers to play an audio recording of the character narrating, also triggered by the motion sensor.

The guests can explore the props inside - riffle through the characters' clothing, read their diaries and letters, examine their possessions, and thereby understand and empathise with their stories.

Design Decisions

Design principles helped us in various stages of our process:

Character selection

Our client and us had different ideas on whether we should be using characters from the musical or shine the spotlight on ones that weren't featured. Building a user journey map made us understand how using new characters would distract the guests from both, our experience, as well as our clients, as they would expect to see these characters in the musical, and also exit during the intermission and search for an interesting character they had seen on stage.

Adding immersive exhibits

The telegram station was designed by Anna Kim.

We wanted means to collect feedback and gauge how immersed guests are without having to fill feedback forms. We thought of a hanging net of messages in bottles exhibit, where we got feedback that it was not period-accurate. We moved on to a postcard exhibit, as the ship was also a mailing ship, but faculty feedback told us that people would not really write a postcard. We then took inspiration from the ship's Marconi room and made our own version of their telegram system, and took the liberty to make it voice-enabled.

Playtesting for guest needs

Playtesting this idea, we realized that guests would feel freer to talk if they knew what they said was anonymous, so we tried iterating different modifications - messages appearing randomly, new ones showing up later, multiple existing messages, until we finally came up with wave-like visuals of messages flowing together, forming an ocean. These were only possible through user research, playtesting, and frequent modifications and feedback sessions.

3D printing and audio interactions

I also designed and 3D printed old telephone receivers for us to attach our speakers to, as playtesting showed that people felt our original, anachronistic phone handsets looked out of place and weren't loud enough.

3D printing our speakers and holders

🤔 Problem

Guests may not place the telephone receivers in the correct orientation with the magnetic sensors, breaking the circuit unintentionally.

🧠 Design Thinking

How do we make orientation irrelevant? The ideal solution is one that works regardless of how users interact with the object.

💡 Solution

Added copper strips all along the receiver and its holder so that the circuit closes no matter which way it's placed.

Testing with (1)magnetic sensors and (2)copper strips as a conductor, to detect when the earpieces are picked up vs. put down

Using the copper strips helped us solve the issue of ensuring people always put down the earpiece in the correct orientation for it to count as "closed" and for the audio to stop. Now, the earpiece can be spun anyway you want, and only reads as "open" when it is lifted up off the holder.

What we learned from guests

It was multisensory. A blind guest was able to enjoy the exhibit by listening, touching and picking up the props, and smelling the different stations. Some guests noticed the different perfume smells and even tried to identify the notes. We added the perfume as a special feature to complete the experience, but we weren't sure guests would notice it.

It reached more people than we intended. It was intended for older audiences who usually watch the plays, but kids enjoyed it just as much, and made sure to handle the props gently.

Guests came back. We anticipated guests would return during the pre-show, intermission and at the end, but faculty were sure they would not. They did, with more curiosity once they had seen the first half of the play and knew the characters.

Guests touched the props. Faculty expected people would not touch the props, much like a museum exhibit, even though we wanted them to. They did, as we expected.
