Little Owl Construction Co.

A physical-digital interactive experience where children collaborate to create an animatronic performance using storytelling, digital art, block-based programming, lighting, and sound, introducing them to the world of entertainment technology.

ROLES: UI/UX DESIGN · EXPERIENCE DESIGN
CLIENT: CMU ETC OUTREACH, EXTENSION, AND ENGAGEMENT DEPARTMENT · CO-DESIGNER: THE BOYS AND GIRLS CLUBS OF WESTERN PENNSYLVANIA · 12 WEEKS

SYSTEMS-LEVEL UX

Overview

Creating a physical interactive platform that utilizes collaborative storytelling to teach middle-schoolers that college is a pathway to the entertainment technology industry.


Concept

Teaching kids to use an animatronic bird to create a performance using storytelling techniques, digital drawing and layout design, block code, basic lighting show control systems, and sound design techniques.

Interfaces

The final interfaces for all five stations: Art, Programming, Lighting, Sound and Facilitator. Each station has its own colour, but they share the same pattern background and block-based visual language so the experience feels like one system.


Art Station

At the Art Station, kids build the backdrop and props for their story on a Huion Kamvas Pro 13 (2.5K) pen display. Touch did not work on the tablet, so every control is designed for a pen.


Scenes as words, assets as images

I first tested icons for the scene backgrounds, thinking pictures would be more visual for kids. We went with text labels like "City" and "Town" because of the small tablet space, and because kids are moving straight from making their story to making the scene, with the words right there to pick from. Assets stay as images, since that is where kids populate the scene with their own imagination.

Programming Station

At the Programming Station, guests use code blocks to design the movement of the animatronic. The station is designed to be similar to other introductory coding programs like Scratch, and each block colour corresponds to a different body part of the animatronic. It runs on a Beetronic 12TS7 tablet.


Wireframe flow

A distinct colour and icon for each movement

Each movement block has its own distinct colour and a UI icon.

Designing head movement

I used Crazy 8s to ideate how guests could move the animatronic's head. Ideas included drop-down boxes for directions or angles, a 3x3 grid for where the head should point, an Xbox controller for live puppeteering (record, puppeteer, stop), separate blocks for head up, down, left and right, two drop-downs for top, center and bottom and for left, center and right, drawing a path for the head to follow, and animated presets like nod, shake, shock, disgust, curiosity and dizzy.

I chose the 3x3 grid, but during UI design we later went with separate blocks for head up, head down, head left and head right, for programming ease.

Paper prototype playtests

The paper prototype was an essential part of the process. It covered the block code, and it's where people suggested loop blocks, where we decided on the head movement blocks, and where we saw how people played with it overall.

Showing where blocks can connect

When a block is picked up, a small highlight circle appears at the connection points of the existing puzzle structure: the bottom of a block for the next block, or, for some blocks, its right end. If the head is already moving up, it can't also move in parallel, so picking up Head Down only allows attaching below the existing structure. Lift Wings can happen at the same time as Head Up or after it, so a glowing circle shows both to the right of Head Up and below it, allowing parallel and consecutive actions.

I tested three variations of this: a ghost jigsaw block showing where the new block can go, a similar shadow effect showing where the new block could land, or just a dot showing where it could be attached. The final version uses the dot.

An expanding loop block

I planned the loop block as an asset that could be reused whenever a new block is attached. The top and bottom pieces stay the same, and an expansion piece is added to make the loop block larger because something new is inside it. Some gap is left inside the loop block at the base, to show the user they can add more blocks there. The loop block is red overall. Loops were later cancelled, so this did not make it into the final build.

Other iterations

Loops. I tested loop blocks that expand as kids add blocks but stay connected to the main chain of blocks, and loop blocks that look like one solid block with the same dimensions as every other block, to make programming easier. The solid blocks would also have a pop-up, and I tested two versions: one with an expanding loop, like the one planned, and one that opens a new overlay window where kids set all their code blocks. Kids didn't use loops in their simple animations, even though adult playtesters from the ETC wanted them.

Pop-ups. Pop-ups were also tested for turn head, wait, loop and move, but overall they were out of scope.

Moving the bird on the platform. I tested two drop-down boxes, one for forward or backward and one for the number of steps, and a digital UI similar to the physical track we would have had. Moving the bird forward and back on the platform was out of scope for the hardware.

Setting eye colour and movement. The eye LED panel was out of scope for the hardware.

Lighting Station

At the Lighting Station, guests use a simple interface to control a pair of DMX lights on either side of the animatronic to light up the bird for the scene. They adjust red, green and blue values, and a code block changes colour to show the current mixed colour, to help them understand how colour mixing works. They then drag the block into the code slots so the lights change through the scene. The lights can be synced with blocks ported from the code in the programming station.

Wireframe flow

Wireframes for lighting station flow.

Paper prototype

Our first iteration was a paper prototype of sliders and a set of saved lighting cues. Guests moved the paper sliders, and facilitators turned on and off three lights with red, green and blue gels attached to a power strip. With on and off control, guests could create only 7 colour combinations: red, green, blue, yellow, cyan, magenta and white.

Unity frontend prototype

The next iteration was a low-fidelity Unity prototype I had created, with the sliders and a panel at the top of the screen showing the current colour created. We still set the three RGB lights manually, since we hadn't worked on show control yet.

We also wanted three different lights, stage left, center and right, to teach kids how to mix lights together in real space too. This idea was scrapped for budget reasons, so only one colour is set at a time.

On and off controls

The team ran into an issue! With the RGB lights we had ordered discontinued, we got white lights instead, and added red, green and blue gels to them. We couldn't control the intensity of RGB, but we could control the intensity of the white lights through the filters. We initially explored toggles because we thought we would just turn the three white lights with gels on and off, giving guests 7 colour options again.

Simplifying the interface

The first interface was confusing, with four sections: a window to see the colour being cast, sliders, a button to create a block, and then a draggable block. We simplified it to the sliders, a window with a button on top to create the block, and then a draggable block. The final design simplified it one step further and removed the interaction to create the block. A block is created directly, with its background showing the colour they have made, and it can be dragged to the scene.

Teaching kids to add blocks

Kids arrive at the Lighting Station from the Art Station, so they drew backdrops earlier and haven't used block code. Kids moving from the Programming Station to the Sound Station are already familiar with block code, so they can already add sounds to their timeline. We added step-by-step tutorial screens at each station accessible through a help button to combat this.

Testing in Unity

We ran the lights through the ETC's show control system. We also tested a dimmer but encountered strobe effects, so the dimmer is set to maximum in the interface and guests can't control it.

We had a build with working sliders in Unity that actively changed the light colours. On the Beetronic monitors, though, we couldn't select or drag the sliders with touch or mouse. The only working solution was to double-click a slider handle and then use the left and right arrow keys on a keyboard to adjust each value slowly. We researched possible causes and tried new builds, including a brand new scene with only a slider, a vertical slider, and disabling unnecessary raycasts, but none of them worked. We decided to change the UI to buttons instead of sliders for the final iteration.

Final design: buttons

The final lighting station replaced the RGB sliders with three visual bars showing the current value of each colour, and a + and - button on either side to adjust it. The buttons change the colour of the light cue block, and also the physical DMX lights in the space.

Connecting the interface to show control

I programmed the lighting station with the help of the show control system. I made the connections between the UI and the backend show control, which eased the programmers' workload, using a C# script that sent the slider/button values to the backend show control system.

This helped ease the programmer's workload in our high-scope project, as we already had 5 total stations to program.

Sound Station

At the Sound Station, kids choose a background track and an ambient soundtrack for their scene, then add sound effects to each movement they programmed. It runs on a Beetronic 12TS7 tablet.


Dropping the recording booth

The station started as a recording booth. Kids could record their own voice lines and attach them to each movement block, alongside the music, ambient sound and sound effects. In playtests, kids were reluctant to voice act, and with a first-person story perspective they didn't say lines as the character either. So we dropped recording and moved to an improv technique called CROW.

Sound station flow, version 1, with a recording booth.

An audio editing timeline

Version 2 borrowed from video and audio editing software, with a timeline for placing sounds.

Emulating physical controls

We weren't fabricating a sound board, so I tried to emulate the controls of a physical sound mixing station with sliders and knobs, to teach kids sound mixing with interesting controls.

Sliders and knobs iterations.

Fitting sound blocks next to movement blocks

Several movement blocks can run at once, like lifting the head while the wings lift, so a sound block had to play during all of them. I tried four approaches:

  1. Using only the first movement block. This misrepresented the work the kids had done.
  2. Using every movement block in the row. This left very little space for the sound blocks kids actually had to add.
  3. Shrinking all the blocks to the size of one. This destroyed the neat block grid and the visual connections between blocks.
  4. Combining each movement's unique colour into one block. This was a new visual that had never been used in the app before.

Block layout trials. Iterations scrapped.

Instead, I moved the whole sound library to the bottom of the screen. That keeps it different from the programming assets on the left, and gives a full-width screen where kids can see programming and sound blocks together. Movement blocks are limited to four at a time (head, wings, beak and wait), so we knew the most space a row of programming blocks could take when carried over to sound.

Designing for loops

Loops were scrapped from the programming station as out of scope, but I still explored how they would work with sound. None of these versions made it into the final build.

Aligning the loop. The loop in the sound area lines up with the loop in the block code, so the programmer can leave space where a loop begins. Kids can add sound effects only to the blocks inside the loop, not to the red loop block itself.

Pop-up versions. In the sound station the loop was compressed from the programming station into a single loop block, without showing its contents. Adding a sound to it, or tapping it, expanded the loop so kids could line sound blocks up with its contents, like a caw for an opening beak that repeats as many times as the loop. Or, for custom sounds, it showed each repetition as its own block instead of one block times four, with a different sound for each.

Numeric drop-down. Adding a sound effect beside a loop block showed a ! on it and a pop-up: "This sound plays over a loop. What would you like to do?" Under Loop Sound, kids could pick how many times. Under Play Once, they could pick whether it plays at the start or the end of the loop.

Simplifying for kids

The final version is simpler: a drop-down for the background track, a drop-down for the ambient soundtrack, and drag and drop sound effects. Background and ambient music are chosen for the whole scene instead of being viewed on a timeline, and the timeline is only for sound effects.

Facilitator Station

The facilitator station is a key part of onboarding and offboarding groups, and it has two functions.
Intro video: the experience opens with a video of the director of the ETC, Derek Ham, introducing it to the students. It plays when the facilitator clicks Start Experience, and when it ends, the experience begins and the movement and backdrop stations become accessible.
Play show: once the lighting and sound stations are complete, Play Show starts the bird's assigned movements and triggers the sound effects to create the guests' final performance.

Other ideas included customising the number of scenes shown at each station based on the story the kids made with the Pixar story prompt. This was scrapped when we moved to CROW.

Scrapped Designs

Start Experience: Other ideas included customising the number of scenes to appear on each station based on the story made by the kids using the Pixar story prompt. When this was scrapped for CROW in the Story Station, the kids would only be making one scene. Additionally, we made this decision to cut down due to time constraints with how long each kid took to complete a station and we had to go with just one scene per station instead of multiple. A bird name field was also intended for use in the UI at certain stations.

Play Show: Initial ideas for a final credits screen would involve the facilitator noting down student names and the roles they partake in before hitting Play Show. This was scrapped due to project scope.
