Overview

I produced Mid-Way, a client project for the Heinz History Center's 2027 exhibit on Western Pennsylvania's amusement park heritage, and directed its narrative. The goal was a playable prototype, and a handoff the museum can reproduce, maintain, and expand without us.

My Role

As external-facing producer, I translated what the museum wanted into work the team could build. As narrative director, I shaped the stories the exhibit tells.

Producer

  • Production planning: alongside my co-producer, Cayla Kennedy, who ran internal production.

Narrative director
  • Learning goals into play: interactive narratives around the museum's objectives, favouring exploration and personal connection over passive reading.
  • Story research: interviews across age groups about fond amusement park memories, as a child and as an adult.
  • Story structure: short factoids per attraction, a 2D-animated personal story as the final reward, and a voiceover added after playtests.

The Design Challenge

The Heinz History Center wanted to make Western Pennsylvania's amusement park history memorable, social, and emotionally resonant across generations. Visitors should move beyond passive viewing and take part in the story, inside one of three planned exhibition themes: Innovation Land, Thrill Factory, or Story World.

Client needs
  • Shared, memorable, multi-generational
  • Emotional connection
  • Active participation
  • Fits one of their three sections
Guest needs
  • Fun and thrilling to learn
  • Curious and proud of local heritage
  • Inspired to preserve memories
  • For kids, career paths in amusement parks
Team goals
  • Digital and physical
  • Not heavy on animation or fabrication

What we built

A table with a simplified park map and four empty slots, each a different shape. Guests place 3D-printed attractions into the slots and a projection responds. Four pieces of one colour unlock that colour's story.

  1. One pieceA grey, old-photo silhouette of the attraction appears, with a short description.
  2. Two of a colourThe LEDs between them light up and the silhouette turns to full colour, as if a memory is evoked.
  3. Three of a colourThe attraction comes alive with animation.
  4. Four of a colourThe full story unfolds: a real memory from someone who visited the park.

Playtesting

I organised playtests with the audiences the museum cares about, coordinating faculty, staff, and the client for the right guests and space. I onboarded guests, took observation notes, ran interviews, and brought the analysis to the team, advisors, and client. Each of the five rounds changed the design.

1. CMU Playtest Nights

Clay prototype · mostly ETC students

Tested
Whether placing pieces was intuitive, using clay attractions and iconography on paper cut to the slot shapes, plus a five-question survey.
Saw
Confusion about how the physical and digital connect, and about what to do first. Players wanted harder puzzle mechanics, which we judged wrong for a quick, low-barrier museum interaction.
Changed
Added trays around the board with the same shapes, so pieces start out and return to a clear place.

2. High school student groups

4 groups · adaptive-controller prototype

Tested
Three stories (purple, green, blue) with recognisable attractions. Groups were onboarded with a scene: it is April 2027, you are at the museum, and you walk up to this table.
Saw
Hesitation at the start, then experimenting. Guests read it as a puzzle or as building a park. They were drawn to the blue pieces because those were laser-cut, not because of the colour.
Changed
Every story gets the same level of finish. Recognisable attractions stayed, since they made the experience more intuitive.

3. ETC Playtest Day

12 groups · cardboard board with RFID

Tested
A full-scale board with RFID readers, with multigenerational and school groups requested from the ETC.
Saw
Guests kept their eyes on the board and looked up only when done. Red and green lights read as "right" and "wrong". Larger groups engaged better than individuals, who overthought the mechanics.
Changed
Moved to pink and green. Added a sound on every placement and a voiceover for stories, because guests preferred listening to reading.

4. Elementary school groups

5 groups · cardboard board with RFID

Tested
Whether young children could understand the goal and the takeaway without guidance.
Saw
Kids fitted pieces quickly and enjoyed it. One group filled every slot but missed the same-colour rule, and some ignored the on-screen text.
Changed
Pointed us toward audio cues and narration instead of text alone.

5. Heinz History Center

14 groups of guests and staff · wooden prototype

Tested
The real audience, on the real floor. Staff also stress-tested it, for example by removing every piece to see if it still ran.
Saw
Guests worked out colour-to-story quickly and most heard a full story. A grandmother told her grandson stories of the old park, and a family played as a group. Some older adults missed the same-colour rule. Discordant sounds made guests feel they were wrong.
Changed
Placement tones are in a major key. A projector placed lower than planned put the screen in guests' sightlines and fixed the split attention. Optional prompts for the same-colour rule are written up for the museum to test.

Handoff

I authored the client handoff for fabrication, install, and maintenance, with the playtest evidence behind each recommendation so choices can be understood, not just followed.

Fabricate
  • Laser-cut layer files and the Blender model for the board, with the workflow from model to cut.
  • Sourced vendors and links for every purchase in both budgets.
  • Print settings, and the materials we tried and rejected, with reasons.
Install
  • One power outlet and strip, a computer, a projector, a screen, and a table of at least 2 by 3 ft.
  • Unity project setup, hardware connections, and startup and shutdown steps.

Maintain
  • The decorative layer lifts off for easy access to the readers and LEDs.
  • Care for high-touch pieces: our 3D-printed PLA was too brittle for a full museum day, so we recommend ABS or injection moulding, with a durable clear coat on painted details.
  • A troubleshooting guide.
Expand
  • Each new story adds four attraction models. The four slots never change.
  • A worked example of adding a story in the Unity project.
  • Narrative guidance: interview themes, a factoid format, and why every story ends with the park's closure.

Public-safety fixes before this goes on a museum floor

  • Round the corners of the slot shapes and model bases, to protect children's hands.
  • Sand or round laser-cut edges to remove burrs.
  • Seal the light openings with a transparent material so fingers cannot go in.
  • Use low-heat LED strips, and add a key lock between the layers, because some visitors lifted the top out of curiosity.

Reflection

This was my first time producing a project this long and my first time managing a client. The hardest part was team dynamics, and realising how I could have detected issues sooner. These notes come from a producer roundtable retrospective workshop run by Stef Harbuck and Charles Johnson.

Keep
  • A physical, on-wall organisation board
  • Talking to teammates one-on-one, especially when dynamics seem off
Stop
  • Individual Miro ideation, then discussion
  • Online scrum boards
  • Fitting 25 hours a week around conflicting electives
Start
  • Asking early when dynamics feel off
  • Ideating together: get ideas out, reinterpret them, tear them apart

Most gratifying
  • Regaining our client's trust after initial misalignment.
  • The team coming together before major milestones, taking on extra roles and reorganising.
  • Getting everyone to open up and solving issues as we talked.
Takeaways
  • Be approachable and reliable. Teammates kept personal issues to themselves because they feared raising them would tear the team apart.
  • Pivot quickly when a tool or method is not working.
  • Deadlines and communication do not have to be weekly.
